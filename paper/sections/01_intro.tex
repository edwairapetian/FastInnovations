\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Submissions to arXiv more than doubled between 2018 and 2025, and in September
2026 the archive received a record 40,363 papers. Large language models now
produce research text faster than any researcher can, and they are starting to
read and summarise for us too. Two opposite hopes and fears follow. Faster
production might flood science with duplicates. Better reading tools might be
the remedy and raise research productivity. This paper argues that free choice
of research topics decides between them, and that what matters about a tool is
not how much time it saves but whom it helps: the people who open new topics,
or the people who join existing ones.

The argument rests on one observation: researchers choose not only how much to
read and produce but \emph{where} to work. Reading has two jobs. It
\emph{teaches}: what one reads raises the quality of one's own work, and more so
in a topic with more to learn from. It \emph{protects}: each earlier paper may
already cover one's project, and reading is how one finds out. Unaware
duplicates lose part of their credit; detected overlaps can be turned into
follow-ons that cite the earlier work, or abandoned. These two jobs make the
payoff of working in a topic rise with the flow of its literature while there is
much to learn, and fall once more of that flow goes unread. Newcomers must also
read a topic's accumulated literature before they can contribute, and they
enter topics until they are no better off than at the frontier, where new
topics are opened by founders who then stay in them.

\paragraph{The results.} Three results organise the paper.

\emph{Frontier incidence.} In a stationary equilibrium every option a
researcher takes is worth the same, so output per researcher equals the value
of opening a new topic (\cref{lem:little}). A research tool is therefore worth,
in the long run, what it adds to that value (\cref{thm:incidence}). Inside a
topic, every speed-up is worth exactly the time it saves (\cref{lem:timeshares}):
because researchers spend little of their time reading, a faster reader gains
little, but per hour saved she gains as much as a faster producer. Entry then
ranks the tools. A tool that speeds up production raises the value of
everything a researcher does, including founding a topic, and survives entry
almost entirely. A tool that speeds up reading reaches the frontier only
through founders, who read in the topics they open. A tool that speeds up
catching up on a literature reaches it least, since founders never have to
catch up. The share of a reading gain that survives entry is at least the
volume elasticity of density, which the data measure, and rises with the share
of the frontier's value that founders earn as incumbents.

\emph{A speed limit.} A newcomer can catch up with a literature only if it
grows more slowly than she can read it (\cref{lem:career}). Topics whose flow
exceeds that speed are closed to newcomers, however rewarding they are, and
incumbents' rents near the limit grow without bound (\cref{thm:speed}). A tool
that lets everyone produce faster raises every topic's flow at once and can
push the densest topics past the limit. Because the stock of literature lags
its flow, topics that grow fast overshoot their best density, even when
newcomers see it coming, and topics that grow too slowly stall before they are
worth joining and are abandoned (\cref{prop:toofast}).

\emph{Units.} Measured by credited output, faster production scales science.
Measured by readership, it adds nothing, because total reading is fixed by
researchers' time and more papers share it; then only reading tools raise
output (\cref{prop:audience}). We report both.

\paragraph{What is new, and what is not.} The equilibrium structure is that of
a system of cities \citep{henderson1974} or of clubs \citep{buchanan1965}. The
average payoff is single peaked in group size, new groups enter freely,
equilibria are stable only on the falling side, the planner sits at the peak
of the average payoff, and an entry tax decentralises the optimum. Self-organised
cities are too large for the same reason \citep{hendersonbecker2000,scotchmer2002}.
We use this structure and claim four things that it does not provide. First,
a microfoundation of the hump in the two jobs of reading, valid for any
learning and detection technology in a broad class. The proof uses joint
log-concavity of the researcher's problem in reading time and density
(\cref{thm:v}), and the hump comes from bounded absorption against a covering
hazard that grows with the literature. Second, the crowders are the readers:
the sign of the spillover from one more paper is derived from the envelope
theorem rather than assumed, and it changes along the payoff curve
(\cref{prop:externality}). Third, the duplication rate is pinned by entry, with
an exact volume elasticity of density built from observables
(\cref{prop:volume}). Fourth, the frontier-incidence theorem and the speed
limit, which together say which research tools scale science and when a
literature grows too fast to join. The catch-up cost, the need to read a
topic's stock before contributing \citep{jones2009}, adds a wedge that the city
literature lacks: incumbents' returns rise with density even where newcomers
find topics too crowded (\cref{cor:overcrowd}).

\paragraph{Evidence.} We confront the model with the arXiv record in two ways.
The descriptive evidence (\cref{sec:facts}) comes from 1.49 million papers posted
in 2018--2025, classified into 150 text-based areas. The anchor fact is that volume
opens new ground. From the 95th to the 25th percentile similarity radius, local
density rises with area volume at an elasticity of 0.12--0.19, against 0.53--0.81
if papers kept landing in the same idea space. At the closest-overlap radius the
estimate is 0.035, though it is imprecise (SE 0.118). Returns are hump-shaped in
topic density. Unaware duplicates lose about a fifth of their citations, close to
the 21\% penalty for scooped teams in \citet{hillstein2025}, while aware
follow-ons gain. Once corrected for citation recall, about 60\% of close overlap
is aware.

The quasi-experimental evidence (\cref{sec:causal}) uses arXiv's own rules on a
new panel of 2.2 million arXiv submissions with exact timestamps through
15~October 2026. Four shocks speak to the margins the model relies on.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Entry cost.} In January 2026, arXiv stopped endorsing new submitters
on the strength of an institutional e-mail address alone; Mathematics had moved
in December 2025. The share of papers by first-time submitters fell by
\empEndOther\ percentage points (about \empEndOtherRel) in the affected
sections, net of the year before. The staggered timing matches the rule.
\item \emph{Catch-up cost.} From November 2025, review and position papers in
computer science needed prior peer review. Review-type papers in computer
science halved relative to other archives, more so in categories that had
relied on them. First-time authorship fell more in those categories, though a
smaller pre-trend of the same sign makes this suggestive.
\item \emph{Writing cap.} From October 2026, each person may submit at most two
papers a month. The cap removed about \empCapRemoved\ of papers. Total volume
kept growing at its usual seasonal pace, so per-submitter caps are a weak lever
on volume.
\item \emph{Readership.} A paper's position in the daily listing depends on how
many others submitted earlier that day. We use this to test the
detection channel directly. Position has only a small effect on citations, and
no detectable effect on later overlapping work or on the share of it that cites
the paper, with tight bounds. Overlap is detected by targeted search rather than
by browsing.
\end{itemize}
At the author level, adopters of LLM-style writing raised their output by about
\empLLMallPct\ relative to non-adopters. That is far less than the 36\% of
\citet{kusumegietal2025}, whose design has been criticised for mechanically
generating positive dynamics \citep{renaultetal2026}.

\paragraph{Magnitudes.} The calibrated model is disciplined by the corrected
arXiv moments and by survey evidence that researchers spend about 7\% of their
working time reading articles \citep{tenopirking2008} (\cref{sec:quant}). At a
given topic, a 30\% faster producer gains \calWriteFixed\ log points and a 30\%
faster reader \calReadFixed: the same per hour saved. In the long run the
producer keeps almost all of it. The reader keeps \calReadPassNoF\ of her gain
if explorers never stay in the topics they open, and \calReadPass\ if they stay
with probability one half; catch-up tools keep \calCatchPass. Per hour saved, a
production tool is worth between \calPerHour\ and \calPerHourNoF\ times a reading
tool. On the simulated life cycle, where founders spend much of their tenure in
topics still growing at a pace set by diffusion, catch-up tools barely reach
the frontier, and the rents of diffusion-limited entry let more of every gain
survive. Valued by readership instead, the ranking reverses: a 30\% faster pen
adds \calAudWrite\ log points and 30\% faster reading \calAudRead. The static
welfare cost of crowding is small: optimal topic choice would raise output per
researcher by \calPlannerGain, and overlap destroys about \calErosion\ of credit
at mature density. Mature topics sit at \calSpeedRatio\ of the speed limit, the
most fertile at \calSpeedRatioTop. With the measured volume elasticity, topic
crowding explains little of the decline in research productivity.

\paragraph{Implications.} \Cref{sec:macro} collects three further results.
Topics that grow faster than a threshold rate overshoot their best density, and
rational entrants overshoot more than myopic ones once a topic grows fast,
because what deters entry is the stock, and a young topic has little of it.
The stepping-on-toes parameter of growth theory has two measurable versions,
depending on whether open topics count as knowledge, and both are close to one.
And policy has to depend on a topic's age. A flat subsidy to the frontier,
optimal in a stationary economy, is almost as large as the margin by which a
newborn topic beats the frontier in the calibration; it raises the bar for the
early followers who carry young topics to maturity, and so kills more of them.
A tax on joining topics in proportion to their stock is zero at birth and
reaches most of the optimum without that side effect (\cref{prop:policy}).

\paragraph{Related literature.} The paper joins five literatures. The first is
the economics of science and priority
\citep{merton1957,merton1961,dasguptadavid1994,stephan1996,hillstein2025,hillstein2025race,bobtcheffetal2017}
and the philosophy of the division of cognitive labour
\citep{kitcher1990,strevens2003}. We add reading as a choice with two
distinct roles, and derive rather than assume the costs of duplication.
\citet{hillstein2025race} show that priority races lower quality; our cost of
crowding works through detection and catch-up instead, and the two are
complementary. \citet{chuevans2021} document that progress slows in large
fields as attention stays with established work, which our speed limit
rationalises. The second is the theory of excess entry, common pools, cities
and clubs
\citep{gordon1954,weitzman1974,mankiwwhinston1986,henderson1974,buchanan1965,hendersonbecker2000,scotchmer2002}
and of patent races \citep{loury1979,dasguptastiglitz1980}. Our resource is
attention to a literature, and the crowders are its readers; spatial models of
endogenous agglomeration \citep{lucasrossihansberg2002,allenarkolakis2014}
suggest how topics could form in a continuous idea space. The third is the
direction of research
\citep{bryanlemus2017,hopenhaynsquintani2021,carnehlschneider2025,bramoullesaintpaul2010,rzhetskyetal2015,fosteretal2015,myers2020,borjasdoran2012,borjasdoran2015,azoulayetal2019}.
\citet{carnehlschneider2025} model the choice of question by novelty and
feasibility. We model the choice by density, derive the hump, and measure the
margin. \citet{borjasdoran2015} document that researchers move in idea space in
response to crowding, which is the margin our model turns on. The fourth is the
macroeconomics of ideas and of access to knowledge
\citep{romer1990,aghionhowitt1992,jones1995,kortum1997,weitzman1998,bloometal2020,jones2009,ekerdtwu2024,furmanstern2011,murrayetal2016,iariaetal2018,biasimoser2021,evans2008,mccabesnyder2015,agrawaletal2016,parketal2023}.
We give stepping on toes an equilibrium foundation and explain when better
access to the literature raises productivity: when it reaches the people who
open new topics. The fifth is artificial intelligence in science
\citep{agrawaletal2019,brynjolfssonetal2019,jones2025ai,duedeetal2026,haoetal2026,liangetal2024,kusumegietal2025}.
\citet{jones2025ai} studies AI in a task-based R\&D production function, and
\citet{duedeetal2026} model LLMs as labour-augmenting technology that shifts
effort across projects. We separate production, entry, reading, search and
catch-up channels by where their gains end up in equilibrium.
Methodologically, our text-based density measures follow
\citet{hobergphillips2016} and \citet{kellyetal2021}, and the listing-position
design builds on \citet{haqueginsparg2009,haqueginsparg2010} and
\citet{feenbergetal2017}.
