\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Submissions to arXiv more than doubled between 2018 and 2025, and large
language models now draft text faster than any researcher can. A natural worry
follows: innovation may be produced faster than it can be absorbed. When the
literature grows faster than people can read it, researchers miss work that
covers their own, duplicate it, and build less on what came before. On this view, cheaper writing
is a mixed blessing and better reading tools are the remedy.

This paper argues that the worry and the remedy both misread how science
adjusts. Researchers choose not only how much to write but \emph{where}. When
the literature grows, they spread out. The rate at which they duplicate each
other is set by that choice of where to work, not by the volume of papers.
The cost of fast innovation therefore does not show up as more duplication. It
shows up in three less visible places. The first is the gains from building on
others' work, which crowding competes away. The second is a catch-up burden that
lets incumbents keep rents and hides overcrowding behind rising returns. The
third is a class of hot topics whose literature grows faster than newcomers can
catch up, and which overshoot their best density.

\paragraph{The model.} Researchers choose a topic and split their time between
writing and reading. Reading has two jobs. It \emph{teaches}: what one reads
raises the quality of one's own work, more so in a topic with more to learn
from. It \emph{protects}: each earlier paper may cover one's project, and reading
is how one finds out. Unaware duplicates lose part of their credit. A detected
overlap can be turned into a follow-on that cites the earlier paper, or
abandoned at a cost. Topics can be joined or opened, and opening one is a
lottery whose odds worsen as fruitful directions are taken. Newcomers to a topic
must first read its stock of literature, the topic-level analogue of the
burden of knowledge of \citet{jones2009}.

\paragraph{Results.} We prove seven sets of results.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{The payoff curve is single peaked} (\cref{thm:v}). The payoff of working
in a topic first rises with the topic's flow of papers, because there is more to
learn from, and then falls, because more covering papers go unread. This holds for
any learning and detection technology without increasing returns to reading
and with diminishing returns to search. The proof rests on joint
log-concavity of the researcher's problem in time and density.
\item \emph{The sign of the spillover is derived} (\cref{prop:externality}). By the
envelope theorem, an extra paper changes each incumbent's payoff by the slope of
the payoff curve. It helps thin topics and hurts dense ones.
\item \emph{Dissipation and pinned overlap} (\cref{prop:eq,prop:volume}). With free
choice, topics fill past the peak until they are no better than the frontier, so
the gains from cumulative knowledge are competed away. Equilibrium density
responds to the size of the community with an elasticity
$\mathcal E=\eta/(\eta D+|\eps_w|)$ that vanishes as the frontier becomes
elastic. Volume opens new topics instead of thickening old ones, and the overlap
rate is pinned by entry, not by volume.
\item \emph{Rents and overcrowding} (\cref{prop:mature,prop:overcrowd}). Catch-up costs protect
incumbents' rents, so returns rise with density across topics. The returns
gradient equals the catch-up premium. Yet at any stable equilibrium the marginal
newcomer \emph{lowers} total output in her topic once the reading she imposes on
future newcomers is counted. Rising returns coexist with overcrowding.
\item \emph{Too fast} (\cref{prop:toofast}). Because the stock lags the flow, a
topic whose literature grows faster than the threshold
$g^*=\rho\Phat/(b_c\ln(\vhat/F))-\delta$ keeps attracting newcomers past its peak,
and stays overcrowded long after the rush. Faster catch-up tools \emph{lower}
the threshold.
\item \emph{Writing AI scales science; reading AI crowds it} (\cref{prop:ai,prop:solow}). Faster drafting multiplies
output almost one for one and leaves duplication unchanged. Better reading,
search or catch-up tools raise density and shrink the number of topics, and with
an elastic frontier they leave output per researcher unchanged: a Solow paradox
for science.
\item \emph{Stepping on toes, measured} (\cref{prop:sot}). The parameter $\lambda$ of
\citet{jones1995} equals $1-\mathcal E|\eps_w|$, the product of the volume
elasticity of density and the slope of the entrant's payoff curve. Crowding buffers
frontier depletion rather than amplifying it.
\end{enumerate}
The planner opens more topics and puts each at the peak of the entrant's payoff
curve, where the writing wedge vanishes. A frontier subsidy decentralises the
optimum. Policies that act only inside topics are dissipated.

\paragraph{Evidence.} We confront the model with seven facts from pre-registered tests on
1.71 million arXiv papers (\cref{sec:facts}). The topic-choice core
survives its sharpest test. When an area's literature grows, the space around a
typical paper fills up with an elasticity of 0.04--0.27, against 0.53--0.89 if
papers kept landing in the same idea space. Full dissipation fails. The same
researcher earns more in denser topics up to about one standard deviation above
the mean, and less beyond it. The model's catch-up cost reproduces this hump:
mature topics sit just below the peak, protected by the catch-up premium, and
fast-growing topics overshoot it. Unaware duplicates lose about a fifth of their
citations and aware follow-ons gain about 30\%, which supports separating the two
kinds of overlap with partial credit. The AI channels can be told apart. Flat
papers per author, more pioneer papers where LLMs spread, a flat aware share and
flat overlap measured by meaning all say that AI has so far acted on science
through entry, not through reading.

\paragraph{Quantification.} The calibrated model (\cref{sec:quant}) puts the gain
from cumulative knowledge that crowding dissipates at \calDissip\ of output per
researcher, and catch-up at \calT\ years of a newcomer's career. Topics growing faster than
\calGstar\ a year overshoot. The volume elasticity of 0.2 implies a frontier
elasticity of \calEta\ and a stepping-on-toes parameter of \calLambda. Topic
crowding therefore accounts for little of the measured decline in research productivity
\citep{bloometal2020}, and the rest must come from falling fertility of new
ideas or from selection.

\paragraph{Related literature.} The paper joins four literatures. The first is the
economics of science and priority \citep{merton1957,merton1961,dasguptadavid1994,stephan1996,hillstein2025}.
We add reading as a choice, with two distinct roles, and derive rather than assume
the costs of duplication. The second is the theory of common-pool resources and
excess entry \citep{gordon1954,weitzman1974,mankiwwhinston1986} and of patent
races \citep{loury1979,dasguptastiglitz1980}. Our resource is attention to a
literature, and the crowders are the readers. This makes the sign of the
externality endogenous and lets catch-up costs turn dissipated rents into
protected ones. The third is the direction of research
\citep{bryanlemus2017,hopenhaynsquintani2021,bramoullesaintpaul2010,rzhetskyetal2015,fosteretal2015,myers2020,borjasdoran2012,azoulayetal2019}.
We locate the inefficiency in the density of topics rather than in their
riskiness, and derive testable signatures from the arXiv record. The fourth is
the macroeconomics of ideas \citep{romer1990,aghionhowitt1992,jones1995,kortum1997,weitzman1998,bloometal2020,jones2009}
and of access to knowledge
\citep{furmanstern2011,murrayetal2016,iariaetal2018,biasimoser2021,evans2008,mccabesnyder2015,chuevans2021,parketal2023}.
We give stepping on toes an equilibrium foundation with observable inputs and
explain why improvements in access to the literature need not raise research
productivity. On artificial intelligence and science
\citep{agrawaletal2019,brynjolfssonetal2019,haoetal2026,liangetal2024}, we
separate writing, entry, reading, search and catch-up channels and show which
ones the data reveal.
