\section{The incidence of research tools}\label{sec:ai}

New tools, artificial intelligence among them, enter the model through
production speed $a$, reading speed $b$, learning $\beta$, search precision
$\sigma$, the cost of pivoting $\kappa_p$, catch-up speed $b_c$, and the cost of
entering science. Inside a topic, \cref{lem:timeshares} prices each speed-up by
the time it saves. Where that value ends up once researchers move is a
question of incidence. \Cref{lem:little} answers it: in the long run, output
per researcher is the value of the frontier.

\begin{theorem}[Frontier incidence]\label{thm:incidence}
Let a tool raise $\ln w$ by $h$ and $\ln F$ by $f$ at a given density, where $f$
includes the frontier's response to any change in the number of topics needed
to hold the researchers at that density. In the large-topic limit,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:incidence}
\dd\ln\frac YN=\dd\ln a+\lambda_F\,f+(1-\lambda_F)\,h,\qquad
\dd\ln P^*=\frac{h-f}{\Psi},\qquad
\lambda_F\equiv\frac{|\eps_w|}{\Psi},\quad \Psi\equiv|\eps_w|+\theta_F\eps_U+\eta_FD.
\end{equation}
In the stationary economy ($\eps_U=\eps_v$) the share of a newcomer-side gain
that survives entry is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:passthrough}
1-\lambda_F=1-\big(1-\mathcal E D\big)\,\frac{|\eps_w|}{|\eps_w|+\theta_F\eps_v},
\end{equation}
which is at least $\mathcal E D$ when mature topics lie on the rising side of $v$.
\end{theorem}

In the long run a tool is worth what it adds to the value of opening a new
topic. A tool that raises payoffs only inside existing topics ($h>0$, $f=0$)
draws newcomers in until those topics are again no better than the frontier.
Its gain survives only through the two channels by which the frontier depends
on density: founders who stay earn the payoffs of the denser topics they end
up in ($\theta_F\eps_U$), and fewer topics leave better directions open at the
frontier ($\eta_FD$). \Cref{eq:passthrough} puts a floor under the surviving
share that the data can see: the volume elasticity of density, scaled by
$D\approx1$. Without founders the floor is exact.

\begin{corollary}[Ranking tools]\label{cor:ordering}
In the large-topic limit:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Production tools} ($a$) act like a larger community: $f=-\eta_F\,\dd\ln a$ and $h=0$, so
\[
\dd\ln(Y/N)/\dd\ln a=1-\mathcal E|\eps_w| .
\]
\item \emph{Learning tools} ($b$, $\beta$, $\sigma$, $\kappa_p$) raise $\ln v$ by $h_v$ at every density. Founders read too, so $f=\theta_F h_v$ up to the small change in the number of topics, and the share that survives is $\Pi_L=1-\lambda_F(1-\theta_F)$.
\item \emph{Catch-up tools} ($b_c$) raise only newcomers' payoffs. Founders never catch up, so $f=0$ up to the same term, and $\Pi_C=1-\lambda_F$.
\item Hence $\Pi_C\le\Pi_L\le1-\mathcal E|\eps_w|$ whenever $\eta_F\le1-\theta_F$. At fixed topics every speed-up is worth the same per unit of time saved (\cref{lem:timeshares}); in the long run, per unit of time saved, a production tool is worth $(1-\mathcal E|\eps_w|)/\Pi_L$ times a reading tool.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

The theorem replaces the dissipation result of earlier versions of this
paper, which had $\theta_F=0$ by construction: pioneers who never read and
never stayed in the topics they opened, so that nothing that happened inside
topics could reach the frontier. Once founders stay, reading tools reach the
frontier directly, because founders read. Catch-up tools reach it only
indirectly, through the density of the topics founders end up in, because
founders never have to catch up. The asymmetry that survives is between tools
that help the people who open topics and tools that help only those who join
them. How
large it is depends on $\theta_F$, the share of the frontier's value that
founders earn as incumbents, which is a measurable object: the share of an
explorer's expected output that comes from her years as the founder of a
topic.

Two further channels can be read off the theorem. If explorers need search to
check that a direction is not already taken, so that their founding rate
rises with search precision at elasticity $\eps_{s\sigma}$, search tools also
raise $f$ by $(\theta_F-\omega)\,\eps_{s\sigma}\,\dd\ln\sigma$, since
$\partial\ln F/\partial\ln s=\theta_F-\omega$. And on the life cycle two
qualifications apply that the stationary theorem does not capture. Founders
spend much of their tenure in topics that are still growing at a pace set by
diffusion, so their payoff responds less to the mature density than $\eps_v$
suggests. And entry into a growing topic is limited by diffusion, so its
members earn rents that entry does not compete away. \Cref{sec:quant}
quantifies both on the simulated life cycle.

\begin{proposition}[Footprints of new research tools]\label{prop:ai}
In the large-topic limit:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Faster production} ($a\uparrow$) acts like a larger community. Density moves with elasticity $\mathcal E$ (zero if $\eta_F=0$) and the number of topics with elasticity $1-D\mathcal E$. Papers per researcher rise with elasticity $1+\eps_x\mathcal E$, and output per researcher with elasticity $1-\mathcal E|\eps_w|$.
\item \emph{Cheaper entry into science} ($c\downarrow$, with $N$ set by $aF=u_0+c$ for an outside option $u_0$, and $\eta_F>0$) raises $N$. Density rises with elasticity $\mathcal E$ with respect to $N$, papers per researcher move with elasticity $\eps_x\mathcal E\approx 0$, and new topics absorb a share $1-D\mathcal E$ of growth.
\item \emph{Better reading, learning or search, or cheaper pivots} ($b$, $\beta$ or $\sigma\uparrow$, $\kappa_p\downarrow$) raise $v$ and $w$ at every density, and $F$ by the founders' share of the gain. Since $\theta_F<1$, density rises and the number of topics falls: science concentrates.
\item \emph{Faster catch-up} ($b_c\uparrow$, for example review articles or literature-summarising tools) raises $w$ at every density and leaves founders' payoffs unchanged, with the effects in (iii). It also raises the speed limit and lowers the overshooting threshold of \cref{prop:toofast}, so more topics grow too fast.
\item \emph{Better search} ($\sigma\uparrow$) raises the aware share $A$ at any given reading coverage.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Parts (iii) and (iv) follow from the envelope theorem. Each parameter raises
$g(r,P)$ at every $(r,P)$, hence raises $v$, or raises $\Gamma$. A pointwise
upward shift of a single-peaked curve moves its falling-side crossing with the
frontier to the right whenever the frontier rises by less. The effect on the
measured overlap rate is ambiguous in general: denser topics generate more
overlap, but better detection removes more of it.

\paragraph{Readership.} The welfare criterion so far is credited output:
papers weighted by their quality and credit. Reads per paper are
$\mathcal A=(b/a)\,r^*/(1-r^*)$, since members read $b\,r^*$ papers each
while producing $a\,x^*$. A faster pen with unchanged reading means each paper
is read less.

\begin{proposition}[Valuing output by readership]\label{prop:audience}
Let output be valued as $Y_\alpha=\mathcal A^\alpha Y$, where $\alpha\in[0,1]$ is
the weight of readership. Then
$\dd\ln(Y_\alpha/N)=\dd\ln(Y/N)+\alpha\,\dd\ln\mathcal A$, and at a given density
$\dd\ln\mathcal A=\dd\ln b-\dd\ln a+\dd\ln[r^*/(1-r^*)]$. Valued by readership
($\alpha=1$), a production tool raises output per researcher by
$-\mathcal E|\eps_w|$ plus the change in $r^*/(1-r^*)$ that comes with density,
approximately nothing, while a reading tool raises it by approximately
$\dd\ln b$. The ranking of \cref{cor:ordering} reverses.
\end{proposition}

Total reading is fixed by researchers' time, and a production tool spreads it
over more papers. If science values papers by how much they are read, only
reading tools raise output, and they raise it nearly one for one. If it values
them by what they contribute whether or not they are read widely, the ranking
of \cref{cor:ordering} applies. Citations sit between the two: they are given
by writers, so total citations grow with the number of papers, but which papers
are cited depends on what was read. We report both units. \Cref{app:perproject}
adds a third qualification. When search for overlap must be repeated for every
project, a production speed-up is worth only the production share of project
time, and it lowers the search done per project, so duplicates rise.

\paragraph{What the data can and cannot yet say.} \Cref{sec:facts,sec:causal}
take the footprints to the arXiv record. At the author level, adopters of
LLM-style writing raised their output by about \empLLMallPct\ relative to
comparable non-adopters, also in fields where AI is not the subject of research
(Fact~\ref{fact:authors}). Because $a$ is research output per unit of
production time, a tool that speeds up drafting raises $a$ only by the share
of production time that goes into drafting, so a small effect on output is
what the model expects from a drafting tool. The area-level evidence on
pioneers and on overlap measured by meaning cannot separate channels: the
first is confounded by changes in wording and the second is underpowered
(\cref{sec:facts}). The reading and catch-up channels remain predictions. The
model says that when literature-summarising tools become widespread, topics
will get denser, fewer new ones will open, more fast-growing topics will
overshoot, and output per researcher will rise by much less than the time the
tools save, unless founders are the ones who use them. The arXiv rule that took
most review articles out of computer science in November 2025 is a first shock
to catch-up costs. \Cref{sec:causal} reports its first stage, and
\cref{sec:predictions} states what the model predicts it will do.
