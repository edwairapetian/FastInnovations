\section{Catch-up costs: rents, overcrowding and topics that grow too fast}\label{sec:catchup}

The frictionless benchmark predicts equal returns across topics. The arXiv data
reject this (Fact~\ref{fact:hump}). The same researcher earns more citations in
denser topics up to about one standard deviation above mean density, and less
beyond it. We now let $\kappa>0$: newcomers must read a topic's stock before
contributing.

\subsection{Mature topics}

\begin{proposition}[Mature topics under catch-up costs]\label{prop:mature}
Let \cref{ass:Q,ass:phi,ass:credit,ass:interior} hold and $\kappa>0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $w(P)=v(P)e^{-\kappa P}$ is strictly log-concave, with a unique peak $\hat P_w<\Phat$.
\item In a topic equilibrium the mature density $P^m$ solves $w(P^m)=F(M)$ on the falling side of $w$. Incumbents earn $a\,v(P^m)$ per period. Their log premium over the frontier equals the catch-up cost, $\ln[v(P^m)/F(M)]=\kappa P^m=\rho T$, where $T=S/b_c$ is catch-up time.
\item Mature topics lie on the \emph{rising} side of $v$ if and only if catch-up is costly relative to the rent at the peak: $\kappa\Phat>\ln(\vhat/F)$.
\item Let topics differ in fertility $\zeta$, so that payoffs are $\zeta v(P)$, and let $P^m(\zeta)$ solve $\zeta w(P)=F$. Across mature topics, log returns rise with log density with slope exactly equal to the premium:
\[
\frac{\dd\ln[\zeta\,v(P^m(\zeta))]}{\dd\ln P^m(\zeta)}=\kappa P^m(\zeta)>0.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Catch-up turns the dissipated rent into a protected one. Incumbents earn more
than the frontier, and the premium exactly offsets what a newcomer gives up
while reading the stock. More fertile topics attract more entrants, have
larger stocks and so protect larger premia. This yields a positive returns-density
gradient across topics, positive whether those topics lie left or right of $\Phat$.
Part (iv) gives the model's reading of the 6.7\% per standard deviation in
Fact~\ref{fact:hump}. Compared across topics, the returns gradient measures the
catch-up premium, not the slope of $v$.

\subsection{Rising returns do not mean topics are too thin}

A natural reading of Fact~\ref{fact:hump} is that 88\% of researcher-years sit
where more density raises returns, so most topics are too thin. The model
says otherwise.

\begin{proposition}[Overcrowding with rising returns]\label{prop:overcrowd}
In every stable topic equilibrium, one more member in a topic of density $P$
changes total output of the other members by $n\,a\,w'(P)\,\dd P/\dd n<0$.
This counts both the spillover on incumbents ($v'$) and the extra catch-up she
imposes on future newcomers ($-\kappa v$). Moreover:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $\kappa\Phat>\ln(\vhat/F)$, then $\hat P_w<P^m<\Phat$. Incumbents' returns rise with density ($v'(P^m)>0$) and the topic is still too crowded ($w'(P^m)<0$).
\item The planner's density is below $P^m$ in every topic (\cref{prop:planner}). The loss of output per researcher in the large-topic limit is $1-F/\hat w$, where $\hat w=w(\hat P_w)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The measured gradient is the incumbent's private return to density. A newcomer
values a topic net of the reading she must do to join it, and so does the
planner. Rising incumbent returns are the footprint of the catch-up rent, not of an
untapped spillover. The welfare sign stays negative everywhere the entry margin is
active. This replaces the hoped-for ``two-sided'' welfare conclusion with a
sharper one: once the catch-up burden on future newcomers is counted, science
is overcrowded at the margin even where crowding does not show up in returns.
Two kinds of topic can be too thin. The first is young topics that information
frictions have not yet filled. The second, if the frontier is attractive
enough, is newborn topics that never find followers (\cref{rem:coordination}).
We turn to young topics next.

\subsection{When innovation is too fast}

The stock lags the flow. A topic whose literature grows fast has a small stock
relative to its current density. Newcomers find it cheap to join, so they keep
joining after the point where a mature topic would close. This is the literal
sense in which innovation can be ``too fast''.

Consider a topic born with flow $P_0$ and an empty stock. While entry pays,
newcomers who learn about the topic join at a rate that makes its flow grow at
the diffusion rate $g$: $P(t)=P_0e^{gt}$ and $\dot S=P-\delta S$. A newcomer's
career value relative to the frontier is $v(P)e^{-\rho S/b_c}/F$, so the topic
stops attracting newcomers at the first date $t_c$ at which
$v(P(t_c))\,e^{-\rho S(t_c)/b_c}=F$. Call $P_c=P(t_c)$ the \emph{stopping density}.

\begin{proposition}[Too fast]\label{prop:toofast}
Suppose $w(P_0)>F$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $P_c\ge P_c^\infty(g)$, where $P_c^\infty(g)$ is the root on the falling side of
\[
v(P)\exp\!\Big(-\kappa\,\frac{\delta}{\delta+g}\,P\Big)=F.
\]
$P_c\to P_c^\infty(g)$ as $P_0\to0$. The function $P_c^\infty$ is continuous and strictly increasing, with $P_c^\infty(0)=P^m$ and $\lim_{g\to\infty}P_c^\infty(g)=\Pbar_F$, the frictionless density where $v=F$.
\item If mature topics sit on the rising side of $v$ ($\kappa\Phat>\ln(\vhat/F)$), every topic that grows faster than
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gstar}
g^*=\delta\left[\frac{\kappa\,\Phat}{\ln(\vhat/F)}-1\right]=\frac{\rho\,\Phat}{b_c\ln(\vhat/F)}-\delta
\end{equation}
overshoots the peak: $P_c>\Phat$, so at the end of its growth spurt each extra paper lowers incumbents' payoffs. As $P_0\to0$, $g>g^*$ is also necessary.
\item After $t_c$, incumbents stay, since $v(P_c)>F$. Under the fast-stock approximation the topic thins at the turnover rate $\rho$ from $P_c$ to $P^m$. It spends $\ln(P_c/\Phat)/\rho$ periods beyond the peak while consolidating.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The proposition does three things. It locates the overcrowded researcher-years: they are in topics
whose literature grew faster than $g^*$, and they persist long after the rush
because incumbents never pay the catch-up cost that deters newcomers. It explains
why the returns curve in Fact~\ref{fact:hump} peaks and falls off at the top
(\cref{sec:quant} shows how much of this the calibrated life cycle reproduces). And it
identifies who sets the speed limit. The threshold $g^*$ falls with catch-up speed
$b_c$, so literature-summarising tools that make catching up cheap make
overshooting \emph{more} common. It rises with turnover $\rho$ and with the
peak density $\Phat$, and falls with the rent $\ln(\vhat/F)$.

\begin{remark}[Coordination at the frontier]\label{rem:coordination}
A lone pioneer earns less than a group, since $v$ rises at first. Can new topics
then stay too thin because nobody wants to be the first follower? With a
continuum of topics and entry that responds to current payoffs, the answer
turns on a single inequality. If $F<v(P_0)$, the first follower of a newborn
topic already beats the frontier, entry snowballs along the rising side, and
no topic is stuck. If $F>v(P_0)$, newborn topics attract no followers, although
a coordinated group would earn $\vhat>F$. Science then holds too many topics
that are too small. This is the only source of genuinely too-thin topics in
the model, and the planner would fix it by seeding followers. The calibrated
model is in the first case: newborn topics pay $v(P_0)$ above $F$
(\cref{sec:quant}).
\end{remark}
