\section{Quantification}\label{sec:quant}

This section puts numbers on the mechanisms. The aim is not to fit every
moment of the arXiv record. It is to check that one parameterisation of the
model reproduces the main facts with plausible primitives, and to read off its
implications for rents, overcrowding, new research tools and growth.

\subsection{Strategy}

We use the functional forms of \cref{sec:model}: harmonic learning
\eqref{eq:Qexample} and exponential detection. Time is measured in years.
\Cref{tab:calibration} lists the parameters. Some are set externally. The
unit of density is fixed by $b=100$ papers read per year at full-time reading.
Careers leave a topic at $\rho=0.10$ (ten-year tenure), the stock's relevance
decays at $\delta=0.25$ (a half-life of about three years), topics become
obsolete at $\xi=0.04$, and a newborn topic produces $P_0=2$ papers a year. The
credit block comes from Fact~\ref{fact:credit} through \cref{lem:credit}. With
exponential follow-on credit and a pivot that costs 30\% of a project, the
$+29\%$ premium of aware follow-ons and the $-19\%$ penalty of unaware
duplicates imply that 31\% of detected overlaps proceed and that detection
averts \(\Delta/\lambda=37\%\) of erosion.

The remaining parameters are calibrated jointly: learning ($\beta,\gamma$),
erosion ($\theta\lambda$), search precision ($\sigma$), the frontier payoff
$F$, catch-up speed $b_c$, and the distributions of diffusion rates and
fertility. The targets are the corrected returns profile of
Fact~\ref{fact:hump}: the gradient per standard deviation of density, the
returns of the sparsest, fourth and densest fifths relative to the middle one
($-11\%$, $+2\%$, $-2\%$), and the 12\% of researcher-years beyond the peak.
We add the recall-corrected aware share of 60\% at mature density
(Fact~\ref{fact:aware}), which disciplines $\sigma$. A soft target puts the
incumbents' log premium $\kappa P^m=\rho T$ at about 0.15, a year and a half of
catch-up per ten-year tenure. Since the measured gradient also reflects
audience, this is generous to the catch-up channel. We simulate the stationary
cross-section of topic life cycles and run the arXiv regressions on it. As in the
data, density is measured with error. Only 58\% of a paper's nearest neighbours
fall in its own fine area. We add classical noise with a standard deviation of
0.3 in log density, which implies a reliability of about 0.8. Researchers who
move between topics supply the identifying variation in an author-fixed-effects
design, and the model has no ability differences. The baseline analogue is
therefore the cross-section of researcher-years. The within-topic analogue,
which uses only life-cycle variation, is a robustness check. The search is a
quasi-random scan of 2,048 points followed by Nelder--Mead from the best points,
with the winner selected on a fine simulation grid (\cref{app:numerics}). The
frontier elasticity $\eta$ is then set from \eqref{eq:eta} with $\mathcal E=0.15$
(Fact~\ref{fact:volume}).

\subsection{Fit}

\Cref{tab:fit} and \cref{fig:test4} compare model and data. The calibrated model
reproduces the gradient (\calLin\ against 6.7\%), the rise from the sparsest
fifth (\calQone\ against $-11\%$), the share of researcher-years beyond the peak
of the payoff curve (\calBeyond\ against 12\%), the aware share at mature
density (\calAware\ against 60\%) and, without targeting it, the location of the
peak (\calZhat\ standard deviations above mean density, against about one). The
incumbents' premium falls short of its soft target, which is generous to the
catch-up channel because the measured gradient also reflects audience.

The model does not reproduce the top of the profile. In the corrected data the
densest fifth earns \emph{less} than the middle ($-2\%$). In the model the
fourth and densest fifths earn \calQfour\ and \calQfive. The source of the miss
is composition. The densest researcher-years in the model include the most
fertile mature topics, whose incumbents earn high returns, alongside the
overshooting topics that pull returns down. Erosion in mature topics is small,
about \calErosion\ of credit, so the payoff curve falls only gently beyond its
peak. Searches that allow faster diffusion find corner solutions in which almost
every topic overshoots, without a lower loss (\cref{app:numerics}). The fall at
the top points to crowding costs in dense topics beyond duplication and catch-up,
or to audience effects that flatter the middle of the distribution. We regard
it as the model's main quantitative failure. \calShareMature\ of
researcher-years are in mature topics, \calShareCons\ in topics consolidating
after a growth spurt, and \calShareGrowth\ in growing topics.

\paragraph{Overcrowding and rents.} The payoff curve peaks at $\Phat=\calPhat$
papers a year. Mature topics sit below the peak, at $P^m=\calPm$, and the
entrant's payoff curve peaks at $\hat P_w=\calPw$. Incumbents earn a premium of
\calPremium\ over the frontier, the reward for \calT\ years of catch-up. Even
with rising incumbent returns ($\eps_v(P^m)=\calEpsV>0$), the entrant's payoff
curve is falling at the mature density ($|\eps_w|=\calEpsW$), so mature topics are
too crowded once the catch-up burden is counted (\cref{cor:overcrowd}). Moving
every topic to the peak of $w$ raises output per researcher by \calPlannerGain\
(\calWPlannerGain\ in the within-topic calibration, which puts more weight on
fast-growing topics). The optimal frontier subsidy is \calSubsidy\ of the
frontier payoff. The static welfare cost of crowding is small. If all credit
lost to overlap were a transfer between racers, the gain would be
\calGainTransfer\ (\cref{prop:social}). The frictionless benchmark, in which catch-up
is free and topics fill to the density where $v=F$, would dissipate
\calDissip\ of output per researcher. That number describes a counterfactual
economy, not the calibrated one.

\paragraph{Writing and reading tools.} \Cref{tab:ai} and \cref{fig:ai} report the
long-run effects of 30\% improvements in each channel. Faster drafting raises
output per researcher by \calWriteOut\ log points: almost one for one, as
\cref{prop:solow} predicts. It raises papers per researcher by as much and
spreads researchers over more topics. Faster reading raises density by
\calReadDens\ log points and shrinks the number of topics, but raises output per
researcher by only \calReadOut\ log points. Faster catch-up is the most powerful
concentrating force. Doubling catch-up speed raises mature density by
\calCatchDens\ log points and lowers the overshooting threshold by
\calCatchGstar\ percentage points, so many more topics grow too fast. Better
search lowers flagged overlap and raises the aware share. Cheaper entry leaves
papers per author and density nearly unchanged and opens new topics.

\paragraph{Fast topics.} The overshooting thresholds are \calGstarExact\ a year
for myopic newcomers and \calGstarRE\ for forward-looking ones, with a
sufficient bound of \calGstarREbound\ (\cref{prop:toofast}). These are fast but
not unusual rates for hot topics on arXiv.

\paragraph{Growth.} Inverting \eqref{eq:eta} with $\mathcal E=0.15$ gives a frontier
elasticity of $\eta=\calEta$, and the stepping-on-toes parameter is
$\lambda_J=1-\mathcal E|\eps_w|=\calLambda$. Over the arXiv range $\mathcal
E\in[0,0.27]$ it lies between \calLambdaLo\ and one. The short-run bias from
topic-age composition (\cref{rem:shortrun}) is $-\calCompBias$ over a
seven-year window, so even the upper end of the long-run range keeps $\lambda_J$
close to one. Topic crowding and access depletion explain little of the
diminishing returns to research found by \citet{bloometal2020}. Their estimates
require $\lambda_J$ well below one, which in our framework must come from
fertility depletion (\cref{app:fertility}) or from selection.
