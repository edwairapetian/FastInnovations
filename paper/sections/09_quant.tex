\section{Quantification}\label{sec:quant}

This section puts numbers on the mechanisms. The aim is not to fit every
moment of the arXiv record. It is to check that one parameterisation of the
model reproduces the main facts with plausible primitives, and to read off its
implications for rents, overcrowding, AI and growth.

\subsection{Strategy}

We use the functional forms of \cref{sec:model}: harmonic learning
\eqref{eq:Qexample} and exponential detection. Time is measured in years.
\Cref{tab:calibration} lists the parameters. Some are set externally. The
unit of density is fixed by $b=100$ papers read per year at full-time reading.
Careers leave a topic at $\rho=0.10$ (ten-year tenure), the stock's relevance
decays at $\delta=0.25$ (a half-life of about three years), topics become
obsolete at $\xi=0.04$, and a newborn topic produces $P_0=2$ papers a year. The
credit block comes from Fact~\ref{fact:credit} through \cref{lem:credit}. With
exponential follow-on credit and a pivot that costs 30\% of a project, the
$+29\%$ premium of aware follow-ons and the $-19\%$ penalty of unaware
duplicates imply that 31\% of detected overlaps proceed and that detection
averts \(\Delta/\lambda=37\%\) of erosion.

The remaining parameters are calibrated jointly: learning ($\beta,\gamma$),
erosion ($\theta\lambda$), search precision ($\sigma$), the frontier payoff
$F$, catch-up speed $b_c$, and the distributions of diffusion rates and
fertility. The targets come from Test 4 (Fact~\ref{fact:hump}): the gradient per standard deviation
of density, the returns of the sparsest, fourth and densest fifths relative to
the middle one, and the 12\% of researcher-years beyond the peak. We add the
aware share of 38\% at mature density (Fact~\ref{fact:aware}), which disciplines $\sigma$. A soft target puts the incumbents' log
premium $\kappa P^m=\rho T$ at about 0.15, a year and a half of catch-up per
ten-year tenure. We simulate the stationary cross-section of topic life cycles
(\cref{prop:toofast}) and run the arXiv regressions on it. As in the data,
density is measured with error. Only 58\% of a paper's nearest neighbours fall
in its own fine area. We add classical noise with a standard deviation of 0.3
in log density, which implies a reliability of about 0.8. Researchers who move between topics
supply the identifying variation in an author-fixed-effects design, and the
model has no ability differences. The baseline analogue is therefore the
cross-section of researcher-years. The within-topic analogue, which uses only
life-cycle variation, is a robustness check. The search is a quasi-random
scan of 2,048 points followed by Nelder--Mead from the best points, with the
winner selected on a fine simulation grid (\cref{app:numerics}).

We do not target the quadratic summary of Test 4. A quadratic in density imposes a
symmetric hump. The model's payoff curve is asymmetric in log density, with a
concave rise as learning saturates and an exponential fall as erosion
accumulates. The arXiv fifths are asymmetric in the same way: $-8\%$, $+5\%$,
$+1\%$.

\subsection{Fit}

\Cref{tab:fit} and \cref{fig:test4} compare model and data. Three features
are reproduced. The rise from the sparsest fifth matches (\calQone\ against
$-8\%$). The share of researcher-years beyond the peak of the payoff curve
matches (\calBeyond\ against 12\%). The peak lies \calZhat\ standard
deviations above mean density, against about one, although its location was
not targeted. The gradient is somewhat flatter than in the data (\calLin\
against $6.7\%$).

The model does not reproduce the sharpness of the hump. The fourth fifth earns
\calQfour\ against $+5\%$. In the baseline the densest fifth earns
\calQfive, more than the fourth, against $+1\%$ in the data. The source of the
miss is composition. In the model the densest researcher-years include the
most fertile mature topics, whose incumbents earn high returns, alongside the
overshooting topics that pull returns down. Author fixed effects in the data do
not remove topic fertility either. The within-topic calibration, which removes
it, matches the densest fifth (\calWQfive) but has an even flatter fourth fifth
(\calWQfour). Erosion at mature densities is small in the calibration: about
\calErosion\ of credit is lost to overlap. So the payoff curve falls only
gently beyond its peak. The data point to stronger crowding at the top than
duplication alone generates, consistent with the memo's observation that
citations reward audience size and so understate crowding costs in dense
topics. We regard the fall at the top as the main quantitative challenge for
the model.

Where are researchers in the calibrated cross-section? \calShareMature\ of
researcher-years are in mature topics, \calShareCons\ in topics consolidating
after a growth spurt, and \calShareGrowth\ in growing topics. Overcrowded
researcher-years sit overwhelmingly in consolidating topics that grew faster
than $g^*=\calGstar$ a year (\cref{fig:test4}, right).

\paragraph{Rents.} The payoff curve peaks at $\Phat=\calPhat$ papers a year.
There, building on others' work raises output by \calGainPeak\ relative to a
newborn topic. Mature topics sit below the peak, at $P^m=\calPm$. Their incumbents
earn a premium of \calPremium\ over the frontier, the reward for
\calT\ years of catch-up. Without catch-up costs, topics would fill to the
density where $v=F$ and the entire rent at the peak, \calRent\ of the frontier
payoff, would be dissipated. That is \calDissip\ of output per researcher.

\paragraph{Overcrowding.} Even with rising incumbent returns ($\eps_v(P^m)=\calEpsV>0$), the
entrant's payoff curve is falling at the mature density ($|\eps_w|=\calEpsW$).
Mature topics are too crowded once the catch-up burden is counted
(\cref{prop:overcrowd}). Moving every topic to the peak of $w$ raises output
per researcher by \calPlannerGain. The optimal frontier subsidy is
\calSubsidy\ of the frontier payoff. The gain is modest because the newcomer's payoff curve
is flat between its peak and the mature density. Most of what free entry
competes away is the rent of the frictionless benchmark.

\paragraph{Growth.} Inverting \eqref{eq:eta} with $\mathcal E=0.2$ gives a frontier elasticity of
$\eta=\calEta$: the supply of new topics is close to perfectly elastic. The
stepping-on-toes parameter is $\lambda_J=1-\mathcal E|\eps_w|=\calLambda$. Over
the arXiv range $\mathcal E\in[0.04,0.27]$ it lies between \calLambdaHi\ and
\calLambdaLo. Topic crowding and access depletion therefore explain only a small part of
the diminishing returns to research found by \citet{bloometal2020}. Their
estimates require $\lambda_J$ well below one, which in our framework must come
from fertility depletion (\cref{app:fertility}) or from selection.

\subsection{AI counterfactuals}

\Cref{tab:ai} (in \cref{sec:ai}) and \cref{fig:ai} report the long-run effects
of 30\% improvements in each channel. Faster drafting raises output per researcher by
\calWriteOut\ log points: almost one for one, as \cref{prop:solow} predicts. It
raises papers per researcher by as much, and spreads researchers over more
topics. Faster reading raises density by \calReadDens\ log points and shrinks the number of
topics, but raises output per researcher by only \calReadOut\ log points: the
dissipation of \cref{prop:solow}. Faster catch-up is the most powerful
concentrating force. Doubling catch-up speed raises mature density by
\calCatchDens\ log points and lowers the overshooting threshold $g^*$ by
\calCatchGstar\ percentage points, so many more topics grow too fast. Better
search lowers flagged overlap and raises the aware share. That is the signature
the arXiv data do not show (Fact~\ref{fact:aware}). Cheaper entry reproduces what
they do show: more topics, flat papers per author and density nearly
unchanged.
