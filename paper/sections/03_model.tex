\section{Model}\label{sec:model}

The economy is stationary and time is continuous. Within a topic the relevant
literature is a flow of papers, so we describe a researcher's choices per unit
of time.

\subsection{Researchers, topics and time}

There is a continuum of topics. Topics that are open are ex ante identical. A
mass $N$ of researchers each has one unit of time per period, split into a
share $x$ spent producing research and a share $r=1-x$ spent reading.
Producing full time yields $a$ projects per period. The parameter $a$ is
research output per unit of production time: doing the research and writing
it up. It is not drafting speed. A tool that drafts text faster raises $a$ in
proportion to the share of production time that goes into drafting. Topic $j$
hosts $n_j$ researchers who produce, and receives a flow of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flow}
P_j = a\,x\,n_j
\end{equation}
papers per period. $P_j$ is the topic's \emph{density}. We call the ratio of
production speed to reading speed, $a/b$, the \emph{pace} of science.

Density is a flow. What a researcher learns from, and what can cover her
project, is her topic's current literature: recent papers, which carry its
current methods and results. What a newcomer must master before she can
contribute is the topic's \emph{stock}, its accumulated relevant literature
(\cref{sec:careers}). The distinction is the one between keeping up with a
field and getting into it. In a stationary topic the stock is proportional to
the flow, so the distinction matters only for topics that grow or shrink.
\Cref{app:stock} lets learning draw on the stock as well.

\subsection{Reading teaches}

A researcher who reads full time reads $b$ papers per period, so a reading
share $r$ yields $R=b\,r$ papers drawn from her topic's flow. What she reads
raises the quality of her own work by the factor $Q(R,P)$.

\begin{assumption}[Learning]\label{ass:Q}
$Q:\R_+^2\to[1,\infty)$ is continuous, twice continuously differentiable on
$\R_{++}^2$, nondecreasing in both arguments, with $Q(0,P)=Q(R,0)=1$ and
$\bar Q(R)\equiv\sup_P Q(R,P)<\infty$ for every $R$. Moreover $\ln Q$ is
jointly concave and supermodular on $\R_+^2$.
\end{assumption}

Nothing is learned without reading or without a literature to read, and what
one can learn is bounded by what one reads. Concavity of $\ln Q$ rules out
increasing returns to reading. Supermodularity says that reading is worth
more in a topic with more to learn from. Our running example is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Qexample}
Q(R,P) = \big(1+\beta\,h(R,P)\big)^{\gamma},\qquad h(R,P)=\frac{RP}{R+P},\qquad \beta>0,\;\gamma\in(0,1].
\end{equation}
The harmonic aggregator $h$ is close to $R$ when the reader samples a small
part of a rich literature and close to $P$ when she reads a thin literature
exhaustively. Because $h$ is concave and homogeneous of degree one, the
example satisfies \cref{ass:Q} (\cref{app:proofs}).

\subsection{Reading protects}

Each earlier paper in the flow \emph{covers} a given project independently
with a small probability, so the number of earlier covering papers is Poisson
with mean $\theta P$. A covering paper is caught only if the researcher finds
it. With reading coverage $c=R/P$ it is caught with probability $\varphi(c)$.

\begin{assumption}[Detection]\label{ass:phi}
$\varphi:\R_+\to[0,1]$ is continuous and twice differentiable on $(0,\infty)$,
with $\varphi(0)=0$, $\varphi'>0$ and $\varphi''<0$.
\end{assumption}

Concavity says that the first papers one screens are the most informative
about overlap. Random reading with replacement gives $\varphi(c)=1-e^{-c}$. Our
example $\varphi(c)=1-e^{-\sigma c}$ indexes \emph{search precision} by
$\sigma$: a better search engine finds covering papers sooner. Our evidence
(\cref{sec:causal}) is that overlap is found by deliberate search rather than
by browsing new announcements. We therefore read $R$ as the part of the
literature a researcher screens, mostly by targeted search, and $\varphi$ as
the result of that search. \Cref{app:perproject} treats search that must be
repeated for every project, in which case production speed no longer drops
out of the payoff curve.

Some overlap cannot be prevented by reading. A competing project that is in
progress elsewhere, and appears only after one's own project has started,
cannot be found in the literature. Such \emph{concurrent} covering projects
arrive at rate $\theta_cP$.

\paragraph{Credit.} Overlap erodes credit multiplicatively. Each earlier
covering paper that goes undetected turns part of the project into a
duplicate and removes a share $\lambda$ of its credit. Each concurrent one
removes a share $\lambda_c$. When an earlier covering paper is caught, the
researcher chooses between two options. She can \emph{pivot} to a fresh
variant at a time cost that leaves a share $1-\kappa_p$. Or she can
\emph{proceed} as a follow-on that cites the earlier paper and keeps a share
$\delta$ drawn from a distribution $G$. She proceeds if $\delta>1-\kappa_p$.
The expected share kept after a detection is
$1-\ell\equiv\E\max\{\delta,1-\kappa_p\}$.

\begin{assumption}[Credit]\label{ass:credit}
$0\le \ell<\lambda\le 1$, $\theta>0$, $0\le\lambda_c\le1$ and $\theta_c\ge0$.
\end{assumption}

Detection is valuable ($\ell<\lambda$), and duplicates need not earn zero
($\lambda<1$ is allowed). The arXiv evidence (Fact~\ref{fact:credit}) requires
both. Credit here is private. \Cref{sec:catchup} separates it from social
value, since part of the credit lost to overlap may be a transfer to the
earlier paper. Let $\Delta\equiv\lambda-\ell$ and $q\equiv 1-G(1-\kappa_p)$, the
share of detections that proceed.

\begin{lemma}[Credit and overlap]\label{lem:credit}
Under \cref{ass:phi,ass:credit}, expected credit per project is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:credit}
C(R,P)=\exp\!\Big\{-\theta P\big[\lambda-\Delta\,\varphi(R/P)\big]-\theta_c\lambda_cP\Big\}.
\end{equation}
Earlier covering papers that the researcher does not pivot away from
(\emph{flagged overlap}) arrive at rate $\theta P\,[1-(1-q)\varphi]$. A share
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aware}
A=\frac{q\,\varphi}{1-(1-q)\,\varphi}
\end{equation}
of them is \emph{aware}: the later paper cites the earlier one. Unaware
duplicates keep a share $1-\lambda$ of credit. Aware follow-ons keep
$\E[\delta\mid\delta>1-\kappa_p]$, which can exceed one.
\end{lemma}

The lemma follows from Poisson thinning. Detected and undetected covering
papers are independent Poisson counts with means $\theta P\varphi$ and
$\theta P(1-\varphi)$, and $\E[t^K]=e^{-\mu(1-t)}$ for $K\sim$ Poisson$(\mu)$.
Only $\Theta\equiv\theta\lambda$, $\Delta/\lambda$ and $\Theta_c\equiv\theta_c\lambda_c$
enter payoffs. The arXiv penalty for unaware duplicates of earlier work
(Fact~\ref{fact:credit}) identifies $\lambda$. The 21\% penalty that
\citet{hillstein2025} estimate for scooped structural-biology teams is a
penalty for losing a race, so it speaks to $\lambda_c$, not to $\lambda$. The
two numbers are close, but they measure different events.

The detection machinery does three jobs in what follows. It maps the
observable aware share and flagged-overlap rate into density, which is what
makes the invariance of overlap in \cref{prop:volume} a testable restriction
and what disciplines $\sigma$ in the calibration. It separates search tools,
which raise the aware share at given density, from other reading tools
(\cref{prop:ai}). And it supplies the falling arm of the payoff curve
(\cref{thm:v}). The incidence results of \cref{sec:ai} do not depend on it:
they hold for any payoff curve.

\subsection{The researcher's problem}

A researcher in a topic of density $P$ earns credit $a\,x\,Q(R,P)\,C(R,P)$ per
period: producing $x$ projects is worth $x$, and each is worth $QC$. Dividing
by the common factor $a$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:g}
g(r,P)\equiv(1-r)\,Q(br,P)\,C(br,P),\qquad v(P)\equiv\max_{r\in[0,1)}g(r,P).
\end{equation}
The \emph{payoff curve} $v$ is the central object of the paper. Production
speed $a$ scales every payoff and so drops out of $v$. The researcher takes
$P$ as given: she ignores both that her papers teach her colleagues and that
they add to what her colleagues must read.

\begin{remark}[Topic size and what density measures]\label{rem:breadth}
A topic has a fixed set of projects: the coverage rate $\theta$ is a property
of the topic and does not shrink when the topic attracts more people. This is
a normalisation, not a restriction, once density is measured locally. Let a
field of breadth $B$ consist of $B$ cells of unit breadth, each receiving a
flow $P/B$, and let researchers read and be covered within their own cell, so
that the coverage rate per paper is $\theta_0/B$. The field is then $B$
independent copies of a unit topic with density $P/B$, and every result below
holds with $P$ replaced by $P/B$. The model's topic is therefore a
neighbourhood of fixed size in idea space, and $P$ is papers per unit of idea
space: the quantity that nearest-neighbour similarity measures. If papers fall
at rate $P/B$ in a $d$-dimensional space, the distance to the $k$-th nearest
earlier paper scales as $(kB/P)^{1/d}$. ``Opening a topic'' means moving into
an empty cell, and the question of whether topics broaden as they grow becomes
the question of how the volume elasticity varies with the similarity radius,
which \cref{sec:facts} measures and \cref{app:nested} derives.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Careers, catch-up and the speed limit}\label{sec:careers}

Careers in a topic end at rate $\rho$ (retirement, a change of field), and
topics become obsolete at rate $\xi$, which releases their members. The
literature a newcomer must master is a stock $S$ that accumulates the flow and
loses relevance at rate $\delta$: $\dot S=P-\delta S$, so $S=P/\delta$ in a
stationary topic. She reads it at speed $b_c$ before she can contribute. This
is the \emph{catch-up} cost, a topic-level version of the burden of knowledge
of \citet{jones2009}. Papers that appear while she reads must be read as well,
so her backlog follows
\begin{equation}\label{eq:backlog}
\dot B=P-\delta B-b_c,\qquad B(0)=S,
\end{equation}
and catch-up ends when the backlog is cleared.

\begin{lemma}[Catch-up and the speed limit]\label{lem:career}
With a constant flow $P$ and an initial stock $S>0$, catch-up takes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:T}
T(P,S)=\frac1\delta\ln\Big(1+\frac{\delta S}{b_c-P}\Big)\quad\text{if }P<b_c,\qquad T(P,S)=\infty\quad\text{if }P\ge b_c.
\end{equation}
In a stationary topic $T(P)=-\delta^{-1}\ln(1-P/b_c)$. A newcomer who joins a
stationary topic of density $P$ and then earns $a\,v(P)$ per period obtains,
per period of her spell in the topic, the expected payoff $a\,w(P)$, where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:w}
w(P)\equiv v(P)\,\Gamma(P),\qquad \Gamma(P)\equiv e^{-(\rho+\xi)T(P)}=\Big(1-\frac P{b_c}\Big)^{\nu},\qquad \nu\equiv\frac{\rho+\xi}\delta .
\end{equation}
A share $(1-P/b_c)^{\rho/\delta}$ of a stationary topic's members have caught
up and produce, so the topic has $n(P)=P\,(1-P/b_c)^{-\rho/\delta}/(a\,x^*(P))$
members, where $x^*(P)$ is the optimal production share.
\end{lemma}

Catch-up is a race between the reader and the literature. To first order in
$P/b_c$ the catch-up time is $P/(\delta b_c)$, the stock divided by the
reading speed. That approximation ignores the papers that arrive during
catch-up and understates $T$, the more so the faster the topic. The exact
version has a \emph{speed limit}: no newcomer ever catches up with a topic
whose literature grows faster than she can read it. \Cref{thm:speed} draws
out its consequences. The frictionless benchmark is $b_c\to\infty$, where
$\Gamma\equiv1$. Incumbents earn $a\,v(P)$ per period; newcomers earn $a\,w(P)$
on average over their spells. Catch-up reading earns admission and nothing
else: it is not counted as learning. \Cref{app:stock} relaxes this.

\subsection{The frontier}\label{sec:frontier}

Instead of joining an open topic, a researcher may explore. An explorer
produces $e$ per unit of production speed per period: exploratory work that
does not found a lasting topic. She founds a viable topic at rate $s$. A
\emph{founder stays} in the topic she opens. She wrote its first papers, so she
needs no catch-up, and she remains an incumbent until her career ends or the
topic dies. Topics are born at rate $sE$, where $E$ is the measure of
explorers, and die at rate $\xi$. In a stationary state births equal deaths:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:births}
s\,E=\xi\,M,
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the measure of open topics. The frontier is a flow, like the
topics it feeds.

\begin{lemma}[The value of the frontier]\label{lem:frontier}
The value of exploring, per unit of production speed and per period of the
spell it starts, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F}
F=(1-\omega)\,e+\omega\,\bar U_F,\qquad \omega\equiv\frac{s}{\rho+\xi+s},
\end{equation}
where $\bar U_F=(\rho+\xi)\,\E\!\int_0^\infty e^{-(\rho+\xi)t}v(P_F(t))\,\dd t$ is
the founder's average payoff along the path $P_F(t)$ of the topic she opens.
\end{lemma}

The share of the frontier's value that founders earn as incumbents,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thetaF}
\theta_F\equiv\frac{\omega\,\bar U_F}{F}\in[0,1),
\end{equation}
is the statistic on which the incidence of research tools turns
(\cref{sec:ai}). A model in which pioneers never read and never stay in their
topics has $\theta_F=0$. As more directions are taken, a fruitful new one is
harder to find \citep{kortum1997,bloometal2020}. We let the frontier's value
fall with the number of open topics at elasticity $\eta_F\ge0$, so that
$F(M,P)=(M/\bar M)^{-\eta_F}\big[(1-\omega)e+\omega\bar U_F(P)\big]$, where the
dependence on $P$ comes from the topics founders end up in. In the stationary
economy a newborn topic reaches the common density $P$ at once, so
$\bar U_F=v(P)$. \Cref{sec:macro} and the quantification put founders on the
simulated life cycle of their topics.

\begin{lemma}[Output equals the frontier]\label{lem:little}
In a stationary equilibrium in which every option a free researcher takes is
worth $F$, output per researcher is exactly $a\,F$: $Y=a\,N\,F$.
\end{lemma}

The lemma is the renewal-reward theorem. Each researcher moves through
spells: exploring and possibly founding, or catching up and producing in an
open topic. The value of an option is the ratio of its expected output to its
expected length, so a researcher's long-run output per period is the common
value $F$ of the options she takes. In a stationary population the
cross-section average equals the time average. \Cref{lem:little} turns every
question about long-run productivity into a question about the frontier.

\subsection{Equilibrium}

\begin{definition}[Topic equilibrium]\label{def:eq}
A stationary topic equilibrium is a density $P^*$ for every open topic, a
production share $x^*$, a measure $M^*$ of open topics and a measure $E^*$ of
explorers such that:
(i) $x^*=x^*(P^*)$ solves \eqref{eq:g};
(ii) newcomers are indifferent between joining any open topic and exploring:
$w(P^*)=F(M^*,P^*)$;
(iii) the allocation is stable under entry: $w'(P^*)<0$;
(iv) births equal deaths, $sE^*=\xi M^*$, and researchers add up: $E^*+M^*n(P^*)=N$.
\end{definition}

Condition (iii) selects allocations that survive the adjustment
$\dot n_j\propto w(P_j)-F$ topic by topic. A single topic is too small to move
$F$, and at a crossing where $w$ rises one more entrant raises the payoff of
entering and draws in more. Topics in equilibrium need not have the same
density a priori, but (ii) and (iii) leave a single admissible density, so they
do. We state comparative statics in the \emph{large-topic limit}, in which
explorers per topic, $\xi/s$, are small relative to members per topic, so that
$M^*\approx N/n(P^*)$. \Cref{app:proofs} gives the general expressions.
