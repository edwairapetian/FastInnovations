\section{Implications: topics that grow too fast, growth and policy}\label{sec:macro}

\subsection{When innovation is too fast}

The stock of literature lags its flow. A topic whose literature grows fast has a
small stock relative to its current density, so joining it is cheap. This
subsection shows that such topics overshoot their best density, that topics
that grow too slowly never take off, and that overshooting does not rely on
newcomers being myopic.

Consider a topic born with flow $P_0$ and an empty stock. Potential newcomers
learn about the topic over time. While entry pays, they join at a rate that
makes the topic's flow grow at the diffusion rate $g$, $P(t)=P_0e^{gt}$, and the
stock follows $\dot S=P-\delta S$ from $S(0)=0$, so that along the growth path
\begin{equation}\label{eq:growthstock}
S(P)=\frac{P}{g+\delta}\Big[1-\Big(\frac{P_0}{P}\Big)^{(g+\delta)/g}\Big]<\frac{P}{g+\delta}.
\end{equation}
Diffusion is the only friction on the speed of entry. Entry stops at the first
date $t_s$ at which the value of joining falls to the frontier value, and
$P_s=P(t_s)$ is the \emph{stopping density}. After $t_s$ no one joins, members
leave at rate $\rho$, and the topic thins. If it stopped above its mature
density, entry resumes when it has thinned to $P^m$. (We use the stationary
stock for that date.) If it stopped below, it stalled in the valley described
next.

Two kinds of newcomer bracket the possibilities. A \emph{myopic} newcomer
expects the flow to stay at its current level, so her value relative to the
frontier is $v(P)\big[1+\delta S/(b_c-P)\big]^{-\nu}/F$ (\cref{lem:career}). A
\emph{forward-looking} newcomer correctly anticipates that the topic will thin
once entry stops. She reads her backlog \eqref{eq:backlog} along the actual
path of the flow and counts payoffs only once it is cleared:
\[
U(P_s,S_s)=(\rho+\xi)\int_{T(P_s,S_s)}^\infty e^{-(\rho+\xi)t}\,v\big(\max\{P_se^{-\rho t},P^m\}\big)\,\dd t,
\]
where $T(P_s,S_s)$ is now her catch-up time when the flow thins. Her value is
$U/F$.

\begin{proposition}[Too slow and too fast]\label{prop:toofast}
Let mature topics lie on the rising side of $v$, with $\Phat<b_c$, and let the
first follower of a newborn topic beat the frontier, $v(P_0)>F$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{(Take-off.)} Under myopic entry the stopping density $P_c(g)$ is nondecreasing in $g$ and below $b_c$ for every $g$. If $w(P_0)<F$, there is a take-off threshold $g_0>0$: topics that grow more slowly stall below $P^m$, in the valley where a stationary topic is not worth joining; topics that grow faster reach at least $P^m$.
\item \emph{(Overshooting.)} A topic overshoots the peak, $P_c(g)>\Phat$, if and only if $g>g^*\equiv\max\{g_0,g_{\hat P}\}$, where $g_{\hat P}$ is the rate at which a myopic newcomer at the peak density still beats the frontier, $\vhat\,[1+\delta S(\Phat)/(b_c-\Phat)]^{-\nu}=F$. It satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gstar}
g_{\hat P}\le g^*_\infty\equiv\frac{\delta\,\Phat}{(b_c-\Phat)\big[(\vhat/F)^{1/\nu}-1\big]}-\delta,
\end{equation}
with equality in the limit of a negligible birth flow, $P_0/\Phat\to0$, in the stock.
\item \emph{(Forward-looking entry.)} A topic that takes off and grows faster than
\[
g^*_{RE}\equiv\delta\Big[\frac{\Phat\,(b_c-P^m)}{P^m\,(b_c-\Phat)}-1\Big]
\]
overshoots the peak under forward-looking entry.
\item \emph{(Consolidation.)} After entry stops above $P^m$, incumbents stay, since their continuation value exceeds $F$. The topic thins at rate $\rho$ to $P^m$ and spends $\ln(P_s/\Phat)/\rho$ periods beyond the peak.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The proposition gives a clean answer to the question whether ``too fast'' is an
equilibrium phenomenon. The friction that produces overshooting is the stock
lag, not myopia. Part (iii) has a simple logic. If entry stopped before the
peak, the future path of the topic would lie between $P^m$ and the peak, where
$v$ is at least $v(P^m)$, and the flow would fall, so a newcomer would read
less than if it stayed put. A newcomer at the stopping date faces a stock of at
most $P/(g+\delta)$. Whenever $g$ exceeds $g^*_{RE}$ her catch-up is shorter
than in a mature topic, so she earns more than a newcomer to a mature topic,
who earns exactly $F$, and entry could not have stopped.

Part (i) is new to this version and follows from the exact catch-up time. A
newborn topic is worth joining because it has no stock. If it grows slowly, its
stock catches up with its flow before the topic is dense enough to be worth
joining in its own right, and entry stalls. In the calibration a stalled topic
is never worth joining again: its flow falls faster than its stock, so it thins
and is abandoned. Topics that grow too slowly die in the valley between their
birth and maturity; topics that grow too fast overshoot. Both are failures of the same coordination, in opposite
directions. In the calibration the take-off threshold is \calGtakeoff\ a year,
the myopic overshooting threshold is \calGstarExact, against a closed-form
bound of \calGstarInf, and forward-looking entry overshoots above
\calGstarRE, with a sufficient bound of \calGstarREbound. A topic that grows at
200\% a year stops at \calRErationalThree\ papers a year with foresight, against
\calREmyopicThree\ without (\cref{fig:lifecycle}), and below the speed limit of
\calBc\ in both cases.

The result locates the overcrowded researcher-years in topics whose literature
grew fast, which persist long after the rush because incumbents never pay the
catch-up cost that deters newcomers. It also identifies who sets the speed
limit. The thresholds fall with catch-up speed $b_c$, so review articles and
literature-summarising tools that make catching up cheap make overshooting
\emph{more} common.

\subsection{Stepping on toes, measured}

Growth models since \citet{jones1995} let the flow of new ideas scale with
researchers as $N^{\lambda_J}$, $\lambda_J<1$, and call $1-\lambda_J$ ``stepping on
toes''. The parameter is reduced form, and what it holds fixed matters. Here
the flow of credited output is $Y=N\,a\,F$ (\cref{lem:little}).

\begin{proposition}[Stepping on toes]\label{prop:sot}
In the large-topic limit,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sot}
\lambda_J^{\rm eq}\equiv\frac{\dd\ln Y}{\dd\ln N}=1-\mathcal E\,|\eps_w(P^*)|,
\qquad
\lambda_J^{M}\equiv\frac{\partial\ln Y}{\partial\ln N}\Big|_{M}=1+\frac{\eps_w(P^*)}{D}.
\end{equation}
$\lambda_J^{\rm eq}$ lets the number of topics adjust; $\lambda_J^M$ holds the
open topics fixed and adds researchers to them. If, in addition, topics opened
later are less fertile, with fertility elasticity $\eta_f\ge0$
(\cref{app:fertility}), the first expression is an upper bound.
\end{proposition}

Jones's parameter holds the stock of knowledge fixed. If the open topics count
as knowledge, the relevant object is $\lambda_J^M$, the return to adding
researchers to the topics that exist, which the slope of the newcomer's payoff
curve alone determines. If topics are a choice, the relevant object is
$\lambda_J^{\rm eq}$, which is built from two observables: the volume
elasticity of density, $\mathcal E$ (Fact~\ref{fact:volume}), and the slope of
the newcomer's payoff curve at equilibrium density, $\eps_w$. Crowding does not
amplify frontier depletion. It buffers it. The density margin absorbs part of
the growth in researchers, so fewer new topics are opened and the frontier
deteriorates more slowly. In the calibration $\lambda_J^{\rm eq}=\calLambda$
and $\lambda_J^M=\calLambdaFixed$. With $\mathcal E$ between 0 and 0.27,
$\lambda_J^{\rm eq}$ lies between \calLambdaLo\ and one (\cref{sec:quant}). On
either definition, topic crowding and access depletion explain little of the
measured decline in research productivity \citep{bloometal2020}. The rest must
come from falling fertility of new topics ($\eta_f$, which density data do not
identify) or from selection on researcher ability \citep{ekerdtwu2024}.

\subsection{Policy}

The policy implications follow from where the rents go. Anything that raises
the payoff of working inside existing topics is competed away unless it also
reaches the people who open new ones. Anything that raises the frontier is
not. In the stationary economy the planner puts each topic at the peak of the
newcomer's payoff curve, and a flat frontier subsidy decentralises the optimum
(\cref{prop:planner}). Topics are not born at their mature density, though, and
the subsidy has a side effect at birth.

\begin{proposition}[Policy that depends on a topic's age]\label{prop:policy}
Consider the life-cycle economy at a given frontier value $F$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item A flat subsidy $s$ to exploring, or a flat tax $s$ on joining open topics, deters the first follower of every newborn topic if $s>v(P_0)-F$. No topic then takes off.
\item An entry tax proportional to the stock a newcomer must read, $\tau S$, is zero in a newborn topic and never deters its first followers. With $\tau^o=\delta(\hat w-F)/\hat P_w$ it puts mature topics at the peak of the newcomer's payoff curve, as in the stationary optimum.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

In the calibration the stationary subsidy, \calSubsidy\ of the frontier value,
is almost as large as the newborn margin, $\ln[v(P_0)/F]=\calNewborn$, so a
flat subsidy would bring the economy to the edge of the regime in which new
topics attract no followers. Even short of that edge it is a blunt instrument.
It raises the bar for every newcomer, including those who carry a young topic
through the valley before it is worth joining in its own right, so the take-off
threshold rises from \calPolLFGtakeoff\ to \calPolSubsidyGtakeoff\ and more
young topics die. With founders who stay, the frontier value itself changes by
\calPolSubsidyF, and output per researcher-year on the life cycle rises by only
\calPolSubsidyGain. The stock-proportional tax is zero at birth and small while
a topic's stock is still small, so it raises the take-off threshold by less, to
\calPolTaxGtakeoff; it brings mature topics close to the peak of the newcomer's
payoff curve and raises output by \calPolTaxGain, more than half of the
stationary planner's gain. In
practice a tax on the stock means credit, grants and prizes that reward
contributions to young topics and to new lines of work more than
contributions to old, crowded ones. Faster catch-up tools work in the opposite
direction: they lower the effective tax on joining crowded topics.
