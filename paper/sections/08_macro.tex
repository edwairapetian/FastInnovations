\section{Implications: topics that grow too fast, and growth}\label{sec:macro}

\subsection{When innovation is too fast}

The stock of literature lags its flow. A topic whose literature grows fast has a
small stock relative to its current density, so joining it is cheap. This
subsection shows that such topics overshoot their best density, and that the
result does not rely on newcomers being myopic.

Consider a topic born with flow $P_0$ and an empty stock. Potential newcomers
learn about the topic over time. While entry pays, they join at a rate that
makes the topic's flow grow at the diffusion rate $g$, $P(t)=P_0e^{gt}$, and the
stock follows $\dot S=P-\delta S$. Diffusion is the only friction on the speed of
entry. Entry stops at the first date $t_s$ at which the value of joining falls to
the frontier payoff, and $P_s=P(t_s)$ is the \emph{stopping density}. After
$t_s$ no one joins, members leave at rate $\rho$, and the topic thins until entry
resumes at the mature density $P^m$. Under the fast-stock approximation of
\cref{prop:mature}, entry resumes when $w(P)=F$.

Two kinds of newcomer bracket the possibilities. A \emph{myopic} newcomer values
the topic at its current payoff, so her value relative to the frontier is
$v(P)e^{-\rho S/b_c}/F$. A \emph{forward-looking} newcomer correctly anticipates
that the topic will consolidate after entry stops. Her expected payoff per period
over a career that starts at density $P$ is
\[
U(P)=\int_0^\infty e^{-u}\,v\big(\max\{Pe^{-u},P^m\}\big)\,\dd u,
\]
where $u=\rho\times$ time. Her value is $U(P)e^{-\rho S/b_c}/F$.

\begin{proposition}[Too fast]\label{prop:toofast}
Let mature topics lie on the rising side of $v$ ($\kappa\Phat>\ln(\vhat/F)$), let
$w(P_0)>F$ and consider the limit $P_0\to0$, in which $S=P/(\delta+g)$ along the
growth path.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{(Myopic entry.)} The stopping density $P_c(g)$ solves $v(P)\exp(-\kappa\delta P/(\delta+g))=F$ on its falling side. It is continuous and strictly increasing in $g$, with $P_c(0)=P^m$ and $\lim_{g\to\infty}P_c(g)=\Pbar_F$, the frictionless density where $v=F$. The topic overshoots the peak, $P_c>\Phat$, if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gstar}
g>g^*\equiv\frac{\rho\,\Phat}{b_c\ln(\vhat/F)}-\delta .
\end{equation}
\item \emph{(Forward-looking entry.)} A topic that grows faster than
\[
g^*_{RE}\equiv\frac{\rho\,\Phat}{b_c\ln\big(v(P^m)/F\big)}-\delta=\delta\Big(\frac{\Phat}{P^m}-1\Big)
\]
overshoots the peak, $P_s>\Phat$. A topic that grows slower than $g^*$ does not. Since $v(P^m)<\vhat$, $g^*<g^*_{RE}$.
\item \emph{(Fast topics overshoot more under foresight.)} $U(P)>v(P)$ whenever $v(P)$ lies below the average of $v$ along the consolidation path from $P$, which holds for every $P$ sufficiently beyond the peak. For such topics forward-looking newcomers keep entering after myopic ones would stop: $P_s>P_c$. As $g\to\infty$, $P_s$ converges to the root of $U(P)=F$, which exceeds the frictionless density $\Pbar_F$.
\item \emph{(Consolidation.)} After entry stops, incumbents stay, since $v(P_s)>F$. The topic thins at rate $\rho$ to $P^m$ and spends $\ln(P_s/\Phat)/\rho$ periods beyond the peak.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The proposition gives a clean answer to the question whether ``too fast'' is an
equilibrium phenomenon. The friction that produces overshooting is the stock lag,
not myopia. Part (ii) has a simple logic. If entry stopped before the peak, the
future path of the topic would lie between $P^m$ and the peak, where $v$ is at
least $v(P^m)$. A newcomer at the stopping date would face a stock of
$P/(\delta+g)$, smaller than a mature topic's $P^m/\delta$ whenever $g$ exceeds
$g^*_{RE}$. She would therefore earn more than a newcomer to a mature topic,
who earns exactly $F$, and entry could not have stopped. Foresight raises the
threshold a little, because a forward-looking newcomer at the peak anticipates
the decline. But it deepens the overshoot of fast topics, because a newcomer to a
topic far beyond its peak anticipates that consolidation will bring it back
towards its best density. With instantaneous diffusion, a newborn topic with no
stock fills past even the frictionless density. In the calibration the
myopic threshold is \calGstarExact\ a year, the forward-looking one
\calGstarRE, and the sufficient bound \calGstarREbound. A topic that grows at
200\% a year stops at \calRErationalThree\ papers a year with foresight, against
\calREmyopicThree\ without (\cref{fig:lifecycle}).

The result locates the overcrowded researcher-years in topics whose literature
grew fast, which persist long after the rush because incumbents never pay the
catch-up cost that deters newcomers. It also identifies who sets the speed
limit. The thresholds fall with catch-up speed $b_c$, so review articles and
literature-summarising tools that make catching up cheap make overshooting
\emph{more} common.

\subsection{Stepping on toes, measured}

Growth models since \citet{jones1995} let the flow of new ideas scale with
researchers as $N^{\lambda_J}$, $\lambda_J<1$, and call $1-\lambda_J$ ``stepping on
toes''. The parameter is reduced form. Here the flow of credited novel output
is $Y=N\,a\,F(M^*(N))$, because in equilibrium every researcher's career payoff
equals the frontier's. (We write $\lambda_J$ to keep $\lambda$ for the credit lost
by an unaware duplicate.)

\begin{proposition}[Stepping on toes]\label{prop:sot}
In the large-topic limit,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sot}
\lambda_J\equiv\frac{\dd\ln Y}{\dd\ln N}=1-\eta\,\frac{\dd\ln M^*}{\dd\ln N}=1-\mathcal E\,|\eps_w(P^*)|.
\end{equation}
If, in addition, topics opened later are less fertile, with fertility elasticity $\eta_f\ge0$ (\cref{app:fertility}), the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:sot} is an upper bound on $\lambda_J$.
\end{proposition}

The formula is a sufficient statistic built from two observable objects: the
volume elasticity of density, $\mathcal E$ (Fact~\ref{fact:volume}), and the
elasticity of the entrant's payoff curve at equilibrium density,
$\eps_w=\eps_v-\kappa P^*$. Crowding does not amplify frontier depletion. It
buffers it. The density margin absorbs part of the growth in researchers, so
fewer new topics are opened and the frontier deteriorates more slowly. The cost
of crowding shows up in the \emph{level} of productivity, not in its elasticity.
With $\mathcal E$ between 0 and 0.27 and the calibrated slope of $w$,
$\lambda_J$ lies between \calLambdaLo\ and one (\cref{sec:quant}). Topic
crowding and access depletion therefore explain little of the measured decline in
research productivity \citep{bloometal2020}. The rest must come from falling
fertility of new topics ($\eta_f$, which density data do not identify) or from
selection on researcher ability \citep{ekerdtwu2024}.

\subsection{Policy}

The policy implications follow from where the rents go. Anything that raises the
payoff of working inside existing topics is competed away: better reading
tools, search engines, summarisers, and caps on writing. Anything that raises the
frontier is not. Writing tools scale science because they scale the frontier
too. The planner opens more topics than free entry does and puts each at the
peak of the entrant's payoff curve. A frontier subsidy of \calSubsidy\ of the
frontier payoff decentralises the optimum (\cref{prop:planner}). In practice that
means credit, grants and prizes for opening new lines of work rather than for
contributing to crowded ones.
