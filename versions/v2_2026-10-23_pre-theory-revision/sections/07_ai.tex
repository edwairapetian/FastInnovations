\section{Faster writing scales science, better reading crowds it}\label{sec:ai}

New tools, artificial intelligence among them, enter the model through several
parameters. Each leaves a different footprint, and the footprints of writing and
reading tools point in opposite directions.

\begin{proposition}[Footprints of new research tools]\label{prop:ai}
In the large-topic limit:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Faster drafting} ($a\uparrow$) acts like a larger community. Density moves with elasticity $\mathcal E$ (zero if $\eta=0$) and the number of topics with elasticity $1-D\mathcal E$. Papers per researcher rise with elasticity $1+\eps_x\mathcal E$, and output per researcher with elasticity $1-\mathcal E|\eps_w|$.
\item \emph{Cheaper entry into science} ($c\downarrow$, with $N$ set by $aF(M)=\omega+c$ and $\eta>0$) raises $N$. Density rises with elasticity $\mathcal E$ with respect to $N$, papers per researcher move with elasticity $\eps_x\mathcal E\approx 0$, and new topics absorb a share $1-D\mathcal E$ of growth.
\item \emph{Better reading, learning or search, or cheaper pivots} ($b$, $\beta$ or $\sigma\uparrow$, $\kappa_p\downarrow$) raise $v$ and $w$ at every density. Density rises and the number of topics falls: science concentrates.
\item \emph{Faster catch-up} ($b_c\uparrow$, for example review articles or literature-summarising tools) raises $w$ at every density, with the effects in (iii). It also lowers the overshooting threshold of \cref{prop:toofast}, so more topics grow too fast.
\item \emph{Better search} ($\sigma\uparrow$) raises the aware share $A$ at any given reading coverage.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Parts (iii) and (iv) follow from the envelope theorem. Each parameter raises
$g(r,P)$ at every $(r,P)$, hence raises $v$. A pointwise upward shift of a
single-peaked curve moves its falling-side crossing with a fixed $F$ to the right.
The effect on the measured overlap rate is ambiguous in general: denser topics
generate more overlap, but better detection removes more of it.
\Cref{tab:ai} reports the calibrated magnitudes.

\begin{proposition}[Reading improvements are dissipated]\label{prop:solow}
Let $\omega$ be any parameter that raises $w$ at every density and leaves $F$
unchanged. In the large-topic limit, $\dd P^*/\dd\omega>0$ and
$\dd M^*/\dd\omega<0$, and
\[
\frac{\dd\ln(Y/N)}{\dd\omega}=-\eta\,\frac{\dd\ln M^*}{\dd\omega}=\eta\left|\frac{\dd\ln M^*}{\dd\omega}\right|\ge0,
\]
with equality if and only if $\eta=0$. By contrast, $\dd\ln(Y/N)/\dd\ln a=1-\mathcal E|\eps_w|$.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{The asymmetry.} Writing speed multiplies every payoff, including the
frontier's. Newcomers spread out, so a faster pen raises output almost one for
one and leaves duplication nearly unchanged in the long run. Reading speed
improves only existing topics. Newcomers crowd in until those topics are no
better than the frontier, so the improvement is competed away. Output per
researcher equals the frontier payoff $aF(M^*)$, and a reading improvement
raises it only because fewer topics are open and the remaining directions are
more fertile, which is a gain of order $\eta$. A reading tool is worth
subsidising, on this criterion, only if it also makes new topics easier to open,
that is, if it raises $F$.

The asymmetry does not require a perfectly elastic frontier. \Cref{prop:solow}
holds for any $\eta$, and the two elasticities can be compared directly. A
proportional improvement in reading that raises $\ln w$ by $\dd\omega$ at every
density raises $\ln P^*$ by $\dd\omega/(\eta D+|\eps_w|)$ and output per
researcher by $\eta D\,\dd\omega/(\eta D+|\eps_w|)=D\mathcal E\,\dd\omega$. The
same proportional improvement in writing raises output per researcher by
$(1-\mathcal E|\eps_w|)\,\dd\omega$. The ratio of the two gains is
$D\mathcal E/(1-\mathcal E|\eps_w|)$, which is small exactly when the volume
elasticity of density is small, as Fact~\ref{fact:volume} finds. The asymmetry
is thus tied to an observable rather than to an assumption about $F$.

The proposition matches \citet{evans2008}, who finds that online access
narrowed the set of papers that get cited, and \citet{haoetal2026}, who find
that AI tools expand individual scientists' impact but contract science's
collective focus. Gains from access to frontier knowledge measured for
individuals or topics \citep{iariaetal2018,biasimoser2021} are real, but in
equilibrium they are partly competed away at the level of science.

\paragraph{What the data can and cannot yet say.} \Cref{sec:facts,sec:causal}
take the footprints to the arXiv record. At the author level, adopters of
LLM-style writing raised their output by about \empLLMallPct\ relative to
comparable non-adopters, also in fields where AI is not the subject of research
(Fact~\ref{fact:authors}). The drafting channel is therefore present but so far
small. The area-level evidence on pioneers and on overlap measured by meaning
cannot separate channels: the first is confounded by changes in wording and the
second is underpowered (\cref{sec:facts}). The reading and catch-up channels
remain predictions. The model says that when literature-summarising tools
become widespread, topics will get denser, fewer new ones will open, more
fast-growing topics will overshoot, and output per researcher will barely rise.
The arXiv rule that took most review articles out of computer science in
November 2025 is a first shock to catch-up costs. \Cref{sec:causal} reports its
first stage, and \cref{sec:predictions} states what the model predicts it will do.
