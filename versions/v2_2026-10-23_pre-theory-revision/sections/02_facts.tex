\section{Evidence I: the arXiv record}\label{sec:facts}

This section documents five facts that discipline the model. The analysis frame
holds 1.49 million arXiv papers posted in 2018--2025 (1.65 million through
mid-2026), classified into 150 text-based fine areas. Volume rose by 122\% over
2018--2025. It is complemented by a sample of 25,400 full-text PDFs for citation
behaviour, by citation counts and reference lists, and, for the validation of
awareness, by curated INSPIRE-HEP reference lists. A paper's \emph{overlap
flag} is set when an earlier paper by another group, posted 3 to 24 months
before, lies above its area's 99th percentile of 2018--19 nearest-neighbour
TF-IDF similarity. The \emph{level} of the flagged rate, about 1\% in the base
period, is a normalisation; its \emph{changes} carry the information.
Text-based similarity follows \citet{hobergphillips2016} and
\citet{kellyetal2021}. TF-IDF and sentence-embedding flags agree on only about
a fifth of flagged papers, so we report which measure each fact uses and do not
lean on either alone. \Cref{tab:facts} collects the numbers. Standard errors are
clustered by author unless noted. Clustering by area would be more conservative
and is part of the replication package.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Fact \arabic*.},ref=\arabic*,leftmargin=*]
\item\label{fact:volume} \emph{Volume opens new ground.} We regress the log
local-neighbour hazard, the rate at which a paper's similarity radius is
populated by earlier papers, on the log of the area's prior two-year volume
with area and year effects. From the 95th to the 25th percentile radius, the
elasticity is 0.12--0.19. If an area's papers kept landing in the same idea
space, randomly thinning each paper's observed neighbour list implies
0.53--0.81 at the same radii. At the 99th percentile radius, the radius of the
overlap flag, the estimate is 0.035 with a standard error of 0.118 (95\%
interval $-0.20$ to $0.27$). In the all-arXiv time series, volume rose 122\%
from 2018 to 2025 while the overlap hazard rose about 13\% (elasticity
0.19--0.27). The model's topic is a cell of fixed size in idea space
(\cref{rem:breadth}), so its density is the close-radius density. We therefore
use $\mathcal E=0.15$ as the baseline and report results over $[0,0.27]$. Broad
radii fill faster than close ones, as new topics are packed into an area's idea
space (prediction P7 in \cref{sec:predictions}).

\item\label{fact:hump} \emph{Returns are hump-shaped in topic density.} Comparing
the same researcher across years spent in denser or sparser topics, with author
and year effects (923,000 author-years), one standard deviation of density goes
with $6.7\%$ more fractional citations per author-year (SE 0.15 percentage
points, clustered by author). Relative to the middle fifth of density, returns
are $-11\%$ in the sparsest fifth, $+2\%$ in the fourth fifth and $-2\%$ in the
densest. The densest researcher-years thus earn less than the middle. A
quadratic puts the peak about one standard deviation above mean density, with
12\% of researcher-years beyond it. The shape survives using the third-closest
paper and restricting to researchers active three or more years. Two caveats
matter for interpretation. First, citations reward audience. A paper with more
close neighbours has more potential citers, which by itself produces a rising
gradient, so the rising arm is an upper bound on the returns to density.
Second, papers per author-year rise slightly with density within author,
$+0.5\%$ per standard deviation (SE 0.1). \Cref{thm:v}(v) predicts a fall. The
discrepancy is small, but it is a failed prediction and we report it. Larger
teams in denser topics are one candidate explanation.

\item\label{fact:credit} \emph{Duplicates lose some credit, aware follow-ons gain.}
Among 2018--2021 papers with at least five references, relative to papers in the
same area-year with the same topic density, \emph{unaware} overlaps (which do not
cite their nearest earlier paper) receive 19\% fewer citations (SE 2). \emph{Aware}
overlaps (which cite it) receive 29\% more (SE 5). The penalty is close to the
21\% citation penalty for scooped teams in structural biology estimated by
\citet{hillstein2025} with a different design and field. The two estimates
validate each other.

\item\label{fact:aware} \emph{About 60\% of close overlap is aware.} In the PDF
sample, the raw share of flagged pairs in which the later paper cites the earlier
one is 35--42\% in every year from 2019 to 2026. PDF matching recovers 63\% of
citations, so the recall-corrected share is about 60\%. Recall is not constant:
arXiv identifiers recovered per PDF rose from 5.0 in 2019 to 8.8 in 2026, and the
raw aware share rose by 3.8 percentage points (SE 1.3) after 2022. Only the
comparison across areas with high and low exposure to large language models is
flat ($-0.5$ pp per standard deviation, SE 1.4). An independent check comes from
high-energy physics, where INSPIRE-HEP curates complete reference lists. Among later
papers by other authors that overlap an earlier paper at the area's 90th
percentile of nearest-neighbour similarity, \empAwareInsNinety\ cite it
(\empAwareInsSevfive\ at the 75th percentile). We calibrate to 60\%.

\item\label{fact:authors} \emph{Growth came mostly from new authors, and drafting
tools raised output per adopter only modestly.} At the area level, papers per
unique author identifier stayed flat while the number of authors grew. An
area-level ratio cannot rule out a drafting effect, because entrants change the
composition of authors. Author identifiers are also unreliable in 2025, when many
were reissued. We therefore build an author-level panel with name-keyed
submitter identities (\cref{sec:causal}). Submitters whose 2023 abstracts adopt
the vocabulary that spread with large language models
\citep{liangetal2024,kobaketal2025} raised their output in 2024--2026 by about
\empLLMallPct\ relative to submitters who did not, and by a similar amount in
fields where AI is not the subject. \citet{kusumegietal2025} report 36\% more
papers for LLM adopters. Their timing rule, which dates adoption by the first
flagged paper, mechanically generates positive dynamics \citep{renaultetal2026}.
Classifying adoption before the outcome window, as we do, avoids this.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What we no longer claim.} An earlier version of this evidence
reported that areas with high LLM uptake produced more pioneer papers, and
that overlap measured by meaning did not fall. Neither survives scrutiny. The
pioneer measure uses the same TF-IDF neighbours that changes in wording distort.
In high-uptake areas, rare words in abstracts rose from 25.4 to 33.0 per abstract
and hyphenated words from 6.9 to 10.5 over 2025--2026. Without detrending against
a pre-existing decline, the pioneer estimate is $-0.8$ pp (SE 0.2), which points to
concentration rather than dispersion. The embedding-based overlap estimate,
$-0.19$ pp (SE 0.24) on a base near 1\%, has a minimum detectable effect at 80\%
power of about 0.67 pp, roughly two thirds of the base. Most model channels move
flagged overlap by 7--30\% (\cref{tab:ai}), so the test cannot discriminate.
Finally, 13 of the 15 highest-uptake areas study AI or computer vision, so area
exposure confounds AI as a tool with AI as a subject. The author-level design in
Fact~\ref{fact:authors} addresses this by restricting to fields where AI is not
the subject.

\paragraph{Mapping to the model.} Fact~\ref{fact:volume} is the volume
elasticity $\mathcal E$ of \cref{prop:volume}. Fact~\ref{fact:hump} rejects the
equal returns of the frictionless model and motivates the catch-up cost of
\cref{sec:catchup}. Fact~\ref{fact:credit} disciplines the credit parameters
$\lambda$ and $\Delta/\lambda$ (\cref{lem:credit}), and Fact~\ref{fact:aware}
disciplines search precision $\sigma$ through the aware share \eqref{eq:aware}.
None of these facts is causal. The next section turns to designs that are.
