\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Is innovation too fast? The model's answer is that the question is usually
asked about the wrong margin. When papers arrive faster than they can be read,
researchers do not pile into the same topics until duplication explodes. They
spread out, and the duplication rate stays where entry pins it. The arXiv record
bears this out. Volume more than doubled while close overlap barely moved,
and the entry margin responds to its cost. The costs of speed lie elsewhere:
in the reading that newcomers must do to join crowded topics, in incumbents'
rents behind that wall of reading, and in topics whose literature grows faster
than newcomers can catch up, which overshoot even when newcomers see it coming.

The asymmetry between writing and reading is the policy-relevant result.
Tools that speed up writing scale the frontier as well as existing topics, so
they raise output almost one for one. Tools that speed up reading, search or
catching up improve only existing topics, and entry competes the gain away
unless the same tools make new topics easier to open. The calibrated
magnitudes are modest. Optimal topic choice would raise output per researcher
by \calPlannerGain, and the asymmetry is large wherever the volume elasticity of
density is small.

Several limits deserve emphasis. Credit is measured by citations, which also
reward audience, so the rising arm of the returns profile is an upper bound on
the catch-up premium. The causal test of the detection channel is a bounded
null for passive visibility. It points to search costs as the right shock, and
we have stated that design in advance rather than run it. The quasi-experiments
on entry and catch-up costs are, so far, first stages with early reduced forms.
And the frontier is a reduced-form lottery. Endogenising it, for instance by
letting pioneers choose how far from existing topics to locate, is the natural
next step and the margin on which the policy conclusion hinges.
